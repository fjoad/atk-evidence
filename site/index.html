Research reproduction notes

Can we reproduce the published results?

We read a paper, implement the method it describes, and compare our results with the published ones. When they differ, we try to understand why.

These pages show that work: the instructions we followed, the choices we had to make, the code we ran, and the evidence for each conclusion. A successful reproduction matters just as much as an unsuccessful one.

Latest: an electricity-theft detector

We rebuilt the simplest autoencoder in Takiddin et al. (2022) and tested it on the named Irish electricity data. This first run did not reproduce the FC-SAE row in Table III.

A follow-up found a stronger, conditional limit. On these prepared inputs, even a detector allowed to choose each Softmax reconstruction using the true label stays below 50.93% balanced accuracy, versus 83% reported. At at most 15% false alarms, detection cannot exceed 9.25%, versus 81%. New weights or seeds cannot fix that gap while the prepared data, output restriction, and mean-squared-error score stay the same.

This does not mean the model does nothing useful: its fitted score makes small beneficial changes and ranks attacks better than the simple controls tested within similar-input-magnitude groups. Different preprocessing, output layers, and scores remain outside the bound. Read the calculation and its limits.

We returned to the paper: Softmax output, MSE scoring, and the 0.58 cutoff are explicit. Two additional normalization readings still cap detection at 29.81% and 33.96%, versus 81%. A Sigmoid output range removes the all-cutoff mathematical exclusion on the complete evaluation, but one genuinely trained small Sigmoid model still reached only 9.75% detection at at most 15% false alarms—or 25.39% when the score rule was reversed. Every cutoff failed for that model and sample. Its loss was still improving, so this is not a universal Sigmoid impossibility result. LSTM-SAE inside the paper's own training time

The paper reports 183 minutes to train LSTM-SAE on the full ISET data, but does not identify its hardware or epoch count. We gave our documented reading of that model the full 183 minutes on one V100-16GB, a favorable single-device choice from hardware available at Texas A&M during the relevant period. The run completed 1.268 epoch-equivalents and all 8,884,989 saved scores passed the independent audit.

We then tested every one of the 7,036,998 distinct cutoff boundaries. At no more than 13% false alarms, the best detection was 7.00% in the paper's score direction and 23.02% after favorably reversing it, versus 85%. Threshold choice cannot rescue these fitted scores.

One full V100 epoch took 143.88 minutes. Ten epochs in our declared completion project to 23.98 hours, or 7.86 of the paper's time budgets; the paper itself does not state ten epochs. Loss was still decreasing, so this is not an unlimited-time impossibility proof. The bounded result is that this documented implementation does not attain the reported LSTM-SAE row within the reported training time, and ordinary extra training has no observed trajectory toward closing the 61.98-point best-case detection gap. How we investigate a paper

Read and rebuild

Start with the complete paper. Connect each important instruction to the code, and say where the text leaves a choice.

Test and compare

Try small checks first. Run the stated experiment, keep the outputs, and compare every reported measure—not just the best one.

Explain the difference

Test possible explanations. Ask what training adds, whether the claimed component matters, and what limits the method can have.

We distinguish three questions: Did the result reproduce? Did the method work for the reason claimed? Could the stated method reach the target? One failed run cannot answer all three. A mathematical limit must state its assumptions; a statistical conclusion must state what was sampled.

Small exploratory experiments help choose the next question. They do not become reproductions just because their results are interesting. We report our own mistakes and preserve earlier attempts.
